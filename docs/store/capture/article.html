Lights in the deep

A night on the water, and what it taught us about the dark

We left the harbor just after sunset. By the time the engine cut out, the shoreline was a thin line of streetlights and the water around the boat had gone completely black. Then someone dragged a hand over the side, and the sea lit up.

The glow came from countless tiny plankton. Each one makes its own cold light through a chemical reaction, a trick biologists call bioluminescence. Disturb the water and the whole patch answers back in flashes of blue.

A slow snowfall

Far below the surface, light of another kind barely exists. What reaches the deep instead is marine snow, a steady drift of organic flakes from the waters above. It can take weeks to reach the bottom, and almost every animal along the way depends on it.

Some of those animals bring their own light with them. The anglerfish dangles a glowing lure in front of its mouth, a partnership with bacteria that is one of the stranger examples of symbiosis in the ocean.

Back on deck

Our guide said the display changes with the season. In late summer the water is warm and crowded with plankton, and a single oar stroke can leave a glowing trail several meters long. In winter you might paddle for an hour and see nothing at all.

By midnight the wind had picked up and the glow faded into ordinary dark water. On the way home we kept trailing our hands over the side, hoping for one more flash.

It never came. But for the rest of the week, every dark stretch of water looked different, as if it were only waiting for someone to reach in.
